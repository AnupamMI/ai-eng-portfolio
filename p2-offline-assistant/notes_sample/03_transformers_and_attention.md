# Transformers and Attention

## 1. From Sequence Models to Transformers
A sequence is an ordered collection of elements, such as words, image patches, audio frames, or time-series measurements. Earlier neural sequence models often processed elements one at a time using recurrent neural networks. Recurrent networks maintain a hidden state that summarizes previous inputs, but sequential computation limits parallelism and long-range information can be difficult to preserve. Transformers replaced recurrence with attention mechanisms that allow each position to combine information from other positions directly.

The central idea is contextual representation. The meaning of a token depends partly on surrounding tokens. In “the animal did not cross the road because it was tired,” resolving “it” requires relating distant words. A self-attention layer computes relationships among positions in the same sequence and uses them to form context-aware representations. Because all positions can be processed in parallel within a layer, transformers are well suited to modern accelerators.

A transformer does not inherently understand language or the world. It learns statistical relationships from training data and its objective. Language models may learn to predict the next token, while encoder models may learn masked-token prediction or task-specific objectives. Their outputs can encode useful structure, but apparent fluency does not guarantee factual correctness, causal reasoning, or reliable calibration.

Transformers are used in text, vision, audio, video, and multimodal systems. In vision transformers, an image is divided into patches, each patch is projected into a vector, and attention operates over the resulting sequence. In document models, tokens may include text and visual features. The architecture's flexibility comes with costs: attention can be expensive for long sequences, and large models require substantial data and compute. Understanding tokenization, embeddings, attention, and optimization is therefore necessary to reason about transformer behavior.

## 2. Tokens, Embeddings, and Positional Information
A transformer operates on vectors rather than raw words. A tokenizer converts text into discrete token identifiers. A token may be a whole word, a subword fragment, punctuation, or a byte-level unit. Subword tokenization allows a fixed vocabulary to represent rare or unseen words as combinations of familiar pieces. Tokenization affects sequence length, vocabulary statistics, and model behavior; a word that appears as several tokens consumes more context positions than a single-token word.

An embedding layer maps each token identifier to a learned vector of a chosen dimension. These vectors are not fixed dictionary definitions. During training, they are adjusted so that the network can use them to predict its objective. The embedding sequence is the initial representation passed into transformer blocks. In some architectures, input and output token embeddings share weights, reducing parameter count and tying the two spaces together.

Self-attention alone does not know the order of tokens. If the input vectors were permuted, attention without positional information would generally produce correspondingly permuted representations. Transformers therefore inject position information. Sinusoidal positional encodings use sine and cosine functions at different frequencies. Learned positional embeddings assign a trainable vector to each position. Relative-position methods encode distances or directional relationships between tokens, and rotary positional embeddings rotate query and key representations according to position.

Position handling is not merely a technical detail. A model trained with a limited context length may behave poorly when given much longer sequences, even if the architecture can technically accept them. Padding and attention masks are also important: padded positions should not be treated as meaningful content. In batched text processing, failing to mask padding can allow attention to irrelevant tokens and corrupt representations.

## 3. Scaled Dot-Product Self-Attention
Self-attention transforms each input representation into a query, a key, and a value through learned linear projections. A query expresses what a position is seeking, a key expresses what a position offers for matching, and a value contains the information that may be passed onward. These are useful analogies, not literal intentions. The model learns projection matrices that make the attention operation useful for its training objective.

For each query, the mechanism computes dot products with keys to produce compatibility scores. The scores are divided by the square root of the key dimension, then passed through softmax to form weights that sum to one across the attended positions. The output is the weighted sum of the value vectors. In words, each position gathers a mixture of information from other positions, with larger attention weights assigning more influence to particular values.

Scaling matters because dot products can grow in magnitude with vector dimension. Large logits can make softmax extremely peaked, producing small gradients for many alternatives. Dividing by the square root of the key dimension helps keep score magnitudes in a manageable range. Attention masks can prevent a position from attending to certain tokens. A causal mask, for example, blocks future positions in an autoregressive language model so that prediction at a given position cannot use later tokens.

Attention weights are sometimes interpreted as explanations, but that interpretation is limited. A high weight indicates how the attention operation mixes values, not necessarily the full causal importance of a token to the final prediction. Residual paths, multiple heads, feed-forward layers, and later blocks also contribute. Attention visualizations are useful diagnostics, not definitive explanations of model reasoning.

## 4. Multi-Head Attention and Transformer Blocks
Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel, each with its own learned projections. Each head can represent a different pattern of relationships, such as local context, long-distance references, or syntactic regularities. The head outputs are concatenated and projected back into the model dimension. Multiple heads increase representational flexibility, although not every head is necessarily distinct or interpretable.

A standard transformer block combines attention with a position-wise feed-forward network. The feed-forward network typically applies a linear transformation, a nonlinear activation such as GELU or a gated activation, and another linear transformation. It expands the feature dimension internally and then projects it back. Attention mixes information across positions; the feed-forward network transforms each position's representation independently. Together they provide both contextual communication and nonlinear feature processing.

Residual connections add a block's input to its output, helping preserve information and support gradient flow. Layer normalization stabilizes the scale and distribution of hidden representations. Some architectures normalize before each sublayer, while others normalize after it. Pre-normalization is common in modern large transformers because it often improves training stability at depth. The exact arrangement affects optimization and should not be assumed interchangeable.

A transformer block is repeated many times to build depth. More layers can increase representational capacity but also raise compute, memory, and optimization demands. Dropout or other regularization may be used depending on data scale and architecture. The residual stream provides a shared pathway through the model, while attention and feed-forward sublayers write transformations into it. Understanding this composition helps explain why changing normalization, activation, or residual placement can affect training even when the attention formula remains unchanged.

## 5. Encoder, Decoder, and Autoregressive Designs
Transformer architectures differ in which attention patterns they allow. An encoder uses bidirectional self-attention, so each position can use information from positions on both sides. This is useful for representation tasks where the complete input is available, such as text classification or extracting contextual features. BERT is a well-known encoder-style model trained with objectives that encourage contextual understanding.

A decoder for autoregressive generation uses causal self-attention. A token can attend to itself and earlier tokens, but not future tokens. During training, the model can process all positions in parallel while applying a causal mask; during generation, it predicts one next token at a time. GPT-style language models use this decoder-only pattern. The model repeatedly samples or selects a token, appends it to the context, and predicts again.

Encoder-decoder transformers contain both an encoder and a decoder. The encoder processes the source sequence bidirectionally. The decoder uses causal self-attention over generated target tokens and cross-attention to the encoder's output. Cross-attention allows target positions to retrieve information from the source representation. This structure is useful for translation, summarization, and other sequence-to-sequence tasks. The original transformer architecture used this arrangement.

Choosing an architecture depends on the task. An encoder can be efficient for understanding a complete input, a decoder is natural for open-ended generation, and encoder-decoder designs are useful when transforming one sequence into another. A common mistake is to compare models without accounting for their objectives, context lengths, training data, or inference procedures. A decoder-only model's ability to generate text does not make it automatically better for every classification or retrieval problem.

## 6. Computational Cost, Context, and Efficiency
Standard self-attention computes interactions between every pair of sequence positions. For a sequence of length n, the attention score matrix contains n squared entries per head. The attention computation therefore has quadratic complexity in sequence length, which becomes expensive for long documents, high-resolution images, or long audio streams. The projections and feed-forward layers also require substantial computation, but the pairwise attention matrix is a key memory bottleneck.

Several methods reduce this cost. Local attention restricts each position to a neighborhood, lowering the number of interactions. Sparse attention uses selected patterns rather than all pairs. Linear-attention methods approximate or reformulate attention to avoid explicitly materializing the full pairwise matrix, though they may change modeling behavior. FlashAttention improves memory efficiency through careful tiling and recomputation while preserving exact attention mathematically; it does not make the fundamental all-pairs relationship disappear.

Autoregressive decoders often use a key-value cache during generation. Previously computed keys and values are retained so the model does not recompute them for every new token. This reduces repeated work but increases memory use as the context grows. Quantization can reduce the memory footprint of weights or caches, while batching can improve throughput but increase latency for an individual request.

Context length is not equivalent to usable memory. A model may accept a long sequence but fail to use all positions equally well. Important information can be diluted, lost in the middle, or obscured by irrelevant content. Retrieval systems, chunking, summarization, and explicit context organization can help. For deployment, measure both prefill cost and token-generation speed, because processing a long prompt and producing a long completion stress different parts of the system.

## 7. Training, Evaluation, and Common Failure Modes
Transformer training commonly uses large datasets and gradient-based optimization. In next-token prediction, the model receives a sequence and is trained to assign high probability to the actual next token at each eligible position. Cross-entropy loss penalizes low probability assigned to the correct token. Teacher forcing supplies the true previous tokens during training, whereas generation conditions on the model's own prior outputs. This difference can contribute to exposure-related errors during long generation.

Training details strongly influence outcomes. Learning-rate warm-up, weight decay, dropout, gradient clipping, normalization, and careful initialization can improve stability. Large-scale language models often use AdamW or related adaptive optimizers. Mixed precision improves hardware efficiency but requires attention to numerical stability. Data quality, duplication, contamination, and imbalance can matter as much as architectural choices. Evaluation sets should be separated from training data and checked for overlap where possible.

Common failures include hallucinated facts, prompt sensitivity, poor calibration, bias inherited from data, and degraded performance on unfamiliar domains. A model may produce confident language even when evidence is absent. Benchmarks can be contaminated by training data, and narrow test scores may not reflect real-world reliability. Evaluation should include representative tasks, adversarial or edge cases, human review where appropriate, and uncertainty-aware handling.

Transformers are powerful function approximators, not guaranteed reasoning engines. Their performance depends on the relationship between training objective, data, architecture, and deployment conditions. In a practical system, retrieval, tools, constraints, and human review may be needed to ground outputs. For vision or medical applications, a transformer prediction should be validated across acquisition devices, populations, and sites rather than trusted because it performs well on an internal random split.
